# Identifiability Guarantees: How to Start

This note summarizes how identifiability guarantees are commonly written in causal discovery papers, especially in the style of work by Kun Zhang, Peter Spirtes, and collaborators.

## The Core Idea

An identifiability guarantee says that the causal object we care about is uniquely determined by the information we are allowed to observe.

Informally:

> If two possible causal models produce the same observable data, then they must agree on the causal object we claim to identify.

Formally, let $\mathcal{M}$ be a model class, let $O(M)$ be the observable information generated by a model $M \in \mathcal{M}$, and let $T(M)$ be the target causal object. The target is identifiable from $O$ if, for any two models $M_1, M_2 \in \mathcal{M}$,

$$
O(M_1) = O(M_2) \implies T(M_1) = T(M_2).
$$

This is the basic shape of most identifiability statements.

## A Standard Theorem Template

```text
Theorem.
Under Assumptions A1-Ak, the target T is identifiable from the observable information O.

Equivalently, for any two models M1 and M2 in the model class, if M1 and M2 induce
the same observable information, then they agree on T.
```

For example:

```text
Theorem.
Assume the data are generated by a linear acyclic causal model with independent
non-Gaussian errors and no latent confounders. Then the causal ordering is
identifiable from the observational distribution.
```

Or, in a weaker form:

```text
Theorem.
Assume the causal Markov and faithfulness assumptions hold. Then the Markov
equivalence class of the underlying DAG is identifiable from the set of conditional
independence relations in the observational distribution.
```

## A Standard Proof Sketch Template

```text
Proof sketch.
Consider any two models M1 and M2 satisfying Assumptions A1-Ak that produce the
same observable information O.

Step 1. Show that O determines some statistical structure S, such as conditional
independences, covariance constraints, residual independences, rank constraints,
or interventional changes.

Step 2. Show that S restricts the set of compatible causal models to a class C.

Step 3. Show that all models in C agree on the target T.

Therefore, M1 and M2 must have T(M1) = T(M2), so T is identifiable.
```

This proof style is useful because identifiability is not primarily about finite-sample estimation. It is about uniqueness at the population level: assuming infinite data and exact knowledge of the observable distribution, is the causal target determined?

## Common Proof Strategies

### 1. Equivalence-Class Argument

Show that the observable distribution determines an equivalence class of causal graphs. Then show that the target is the same across every graph in that class.

This is common in Spirtes-style constraint-based causal discovery. Under assumptions such as the causal Markov condition and faithfulness, conditional independences determine a Markov equivalence class rather than a single DAG.

The guarantee often looks like:

$$
P(M_1) = P(M_2) \implies [G_1] = [G_2],
$$

where $[G]$ denotes a graph equivalence class.

### 2. Constructive Argument

Give a procedure that recovers the target from observable quantities. If the procedure is well-defined at the population level, then the target is identifiable.

The guarantee often looks like:

$$
T(M) = f(O(M)),
$$

where $f$ is an explicit rule, algorithm, or formula.

This style appears in papers that use covariance structure, rank constraints, non-Gaussianity, residual independence, or intervention changes.

### 3. Contradiction Argument

Assume two models generate the same observations but disagree on the target. Then derive a contradiction with one of the assumptions.

The guarantee often looks like:

$$
O(M_1) = O(M_2) \text{ and } T(M_1) \neq T(M_2)
$$

cannot both hold under the assumptions.

This is common in functional causal model papers, where the forward direction satisfies a structural restriction but the reverse direction usually does not.

## What Zhang and Spirtes-Style Papers Emphasize

### Kun Zhang-Style Identifiability

Zhang's work often uses structural restrictions that make causal direction or latent structure identifiable. Examples include nonlinear functional forms, additive or post-nonlinear noise models, non-Gaussianity, measurement-error structure, and rank constraints.

The important writing pattern is:

1. Define a restricted model class.
2. State exactly what is observable.
3. State what target is identifiable.
4. List exceptions or failure cases.
5. Prove that only one causal explanation satisfies the restrictions, except in those cases.

For example, Zhang and Hyvarinen's post-nonlinear causal model paper argues that the model is identifiable in most cases and explicitly studies non-identifiable exceptions.

### Peter Spirtes-Style Identifiability

Spirtes-style causal discovery often focuses on what can be recovered from conditional independence information under assumptions such as causal Markov, faithfulness, and causal sufficiency.

The important writing pattern is:

1. Define the graph class, such as DAGs, MAGs, or PAGs.
2. Define the statistical information, usually conditional independence relations.
3. State assumptions linking graph structure to statistical independence.
4. Show that an algorithm or graphical criterion recovers the correct equivalence class.
5. Be explicit that full graph recovery may be impossible; often only an equivalence class is identifiable.

This style is useful when the data do not contain enough information to orient every edge uniquely.

## A Practical Template for Epigenomics

A modest and defensible identifiability claim for epigenomics might look like this:

```text
Assumptions.
A1. The measured variables are generated by an acyclic causal model over regulatory
    features and gene expression.
A2. The observed variables are noisy measurements of underlying biological states.
A3. The noise terms satisfy specified independence assumptions.
A4. The distribution is faithful to the underlying graph, or satisfies a weaker
    condition appropriate for the chosen algorithm.
A5. Interventions, perturbations, or multiple environments affect only specified
    mechanisms.

Target.
We aim to identify a Markov equivalence class, a set of invariant parent candidates,
or a subset of directed regulatory effects.

Claim.
Under A1-A5, the target is identifiable from the observed data distribution together
with the available perturbation or multi-environment information.

Proof sketch.
First, the observed distribution determines a set of conditional independence,
covariance, rank, or invariance constraints. Second, under the assumptions, these
constraints restrict the compatible causal models to a specific equivalence class.
Third, every model in that class agrees on the stated target. Therefore the target
is identifiable.
```

## Basic Vocabulary

### Identifiability

Identifiability asks whether the causal target is uniquely determined by the available information.

It does not ask whether a finite-sample algorithm works well. It asks whether the target is recoverable in principle if we had perfect population-level information.

Why it matters: without identifiability, no amount of data can uniquely recover the target under the stated assumptions.

### Model Class

A model class is the set of causal models you are willing to consider.

Examples:

- Linear Gaussian DAG models.
- Linear non-Gaussian acyclic models.
- Additive noise models.
- Post-nonlinear causal models.
- DAGs with no latent confounders.
- Graphs with latent variables and measurement error.

Why it matters: identifiability is always relative to a model class. A target may be identifiable in a restricted model class but not identifiable in a broader one.

For example, causal direction is usually not identifiable from purely observational data in arbitrary linear Gaussian DAGs. But it may become identifiable if we assume non-Gaussian noise or nonlinear additive noise.

### Observable Information

Observable information is the information we assume can be learned from data.

Common examples:

- The full observational distribution $P(X)$.
- A covariance matrix $\Sigma$.
- Conditional independence relations.
- Interventional distributions such as $P(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(X=x))$.
- Changes across environments or perturbation conditions.

Why it matters: different observable information supports different guarantees. Covariance gives second-order relationships. The full distribution gives more information. Interventions give much stronger causal information.

### Observational Distribution

The observational distribution is the joint distribution of variables when we only observe the system passively:

$$
P(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_p).
$$

Why it matters: many causal discovery algorithms try to infer structure from observational data alone. But observational data often identify only an equivalence class, not a unique causal graph.

### Covariance

The covariance matrix records pairwise linear association among variables:

$$
\Sigma_{ij} = \mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_j).
$$

Why it matters: in linear models, covariance contains important information about paths, confounding, and sometimes latent structure. Some identifiability results use covariance rank constraints or factor-analysis-like arguments.

Limitation: covariance only captures second-order linear relationships. It may miss nonlinear or higher-order structure.

### Intervention

An intervention changes the data-generating process by forcing or perturbing a variable or mechanism.

For example, setting $X$ externally is written:

$$
\mathrm{do}(X=x).
$$

Why it matters: interventions can distinguish causal direction. If changing chromatin accessibility at a locus changes expression of a gene, that provides stronger evidence than mere correlation.

### Conditional Independence

Conditional independence means that two variables become independent after conditioning on a third variable or set of variables:

$$
X \perp Y \mid Z.
$$

This means that once $Z$ is known, $X$ contains no additional statistical information about $Y$.

Why it matters: many graph structures imply specific conditional independences. For example, in a chain

$$
X \to Z \to Y,
$$

we often expect

$$
X \perp Y \mid Z.
$$

Constraint-based methods such as PC and FCI use these patterns to infer graph structure.

### Causal Markov Condition

The causal Markov condition says that each variable is independent of its non-effects after conditioning on its direct causes.

Informally, once we know a variable's parents, the variable does not need information from the rest of its non-descendants.

Why it matters: it links graph structure to statistical independence. Without such a link, independences in the data would not tell us much about causal graph structure.

### Faithfulness

Faithfulness says that all and only the independences in the distribution are explained by the graph.

In other words, if an independence appears in the data, it should correspond to a graphical separation, not to accidental parameter cancellation.

Why it matters: without faithfulness, the graph may contain paths that cancel exactly, making connected variables look independent. Then conditional independence tests can mislead causal discovery.

### Causal Sufficiency

Causal sufficiency means that there are no unobserved common causes of the observed variables.

Why it matters: if hidden confounders exist, two variables may be associated because of an unmeasured common cause rather than a direct causal edge.

In epigenomics, causal sufficiency is often questionable because cell state, batch effects, environment, genotype, or unmeasured regulators may influence many measured variables.

### DAG

A DAG is a directed acyclic graph. It has directed arrows and no directed cycles.

Example:

$$
X \to Y \to Z.
$$

Why it matters: many causal models use DAGs because they give a clean language for parents, descendants, interventions, and conditional independence.

### Markov Equivalence Class

A Markov equivalence class is a set of graphs that imply the same conditional independence relations.

Why it matters: observational data often cannot distinguish graphs in the same equivalence class. So a more honest guarantee may say that we identify the equivalence class, not the exact graph.

For example, under purely observational conditional-independence information, these two graphs may sometimes be indistinguishable:

$$
X \to Y \to Z
$$

and

$$
X \leftarrow Y \leftarrow Z.
$$

### Target

The target is the causal object you want to identify.

Examples:

- The full causal graph.
- A Markov equivalence class.
- A causal ordering.
- A parent set for each gene.
- A directed edge $X \to Y$.
- A causal effect $P(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(X=x))$.
- A structural coefficient.

Why it matters: different targets require different assumptions. Identifying one edge is easier than identifying the full graph.

### Population-Level Guarantee

A population-level guarantee assumes access to exact distributions or exact statistical relationships, as if the sample size were infinite.

Why it matters: identifiability is usually a population-level concept. Estimation is the separate question of whether an algorithm can recover the target reliably from finite noisy data.

### Estimation

Estimation is the finite-data problem of learning the target from a sample.

Why it matters: even if a target is identifiable, it may be hard to estimate in practice because of small sample size, noise, missing data, weak signals, or model misspecification.

### Proof Sketch

A proof sketch is a high-level version of the proof that explains the main logic without every technical detail.

Why it matters: proof sketches help readers understand why the guarantee is plausible. They usually identify the key assumptions, the observable signal, and the reason alternative causal explanations are ruled out.
